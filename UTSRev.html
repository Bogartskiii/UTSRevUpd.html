Part 1 · Philosophical Perspective on the Self

What Is Philosophy?

You should be able to: explain the foundational concepts and purpose of philosophy; differentiate philosophical perspectives on the nature of the self; construct a personal view of the self grounded in selected perspectives.
  • Etymology: philo = loving + sophia = knowledge, wisdom. Philosophy is the pursuit of wisdom, truth and knowledge.
  • Why study it? To deepen understanding; by doing philosophy we learn to think better, act more wisely, and so improve the quality of all our lives.
  • Philosophy is not primarily the study of other people's ideas. It is first the attempt to state our own views clearly, convincingly and interestingly.
  • Big questions: What is the meaning of life? Does God exist? Do we have free will? What is beauty? What is a just society? And the central one here: Who am I?

Thought experiment: Ship of Theseus

An ancient ship in a museum slowly rots, and its planks are replaced with identical new ones until every part has been replaced. Is it still the same ship, or a completely different one? (This is the problem of personal identity: are you the same person after all your cells and experiences change?)

Thinkers covered

Socrates · Plato · St. Augustine · René Descartes · John Locke · David Hume · Immanuel Kant · Sigmund Freud · Gilbert Ryle · Paul Churchland · Maurice Merleau-Ponty

Socrates

  • Known as the Father of Western Philosophy. Developed the Socratic Method: dialectical questioning that stimulates critical thinking and uncovers knowledge by asking and answering questions.
  • Soul (psyche) is the key to his idea of the self. The soul is immortal; death is not the end but the separation of the soul from the body.
  • The soul is the true essence of a person, not the body, possessions or social status. Caring for the soul is the main task of philosophy.
  • "Know thyself": the first step in caring for the soul is self-knowledge, through introspection and critical self-examination, essential to human flourishing.
  • The purpose of life is the good life, possible only when the soul is in good condition, cultivated through knowledge, wisdom and virtue.
“The unexamined life is not worth living.”

True self = the virtuous self: defined by moral character, not by wealth, power or appearance.

Plato

  • Socrates's most famous student; founded Plato's Academy, the first institution of higher learning in the Western world.
  • The human person: a dichotomy. Body = material, visible, destructible. Soul = immaterial, invisible, indestructible. The soul is the true essence; the body is a temporary vessel that hinders the soul from grasping truth and wisdom.
  • Theory of Forms: the physical world we sense is not true reality, only a shadow or imitation of a higher, eternal realm of perfect, non-material Forms (Ideas).
  • Allegory of the Cave: prisoners are chained in a dark cave watching shadows on a wall cast by objects behind them. The shadows = the material world that most people mistake for reality. The real objects = the Forms, true reality outside the cave, which only the philosopher perceives by turning toward the light of reason.

Tripartite theory of the soul

PartRole
RationalThinking, analyzing, decision-making; seeks truth and wisdom
SpiritedSource of emotions such as courage, anger, ambition; sense of honor
AppetitivePhysical desires: hunger, thirst, sexual urges
  • Allegory of the Chariot: the soul is a chariot driven by a charioteer (reason) controlling two horses: one noble and spirited (will/emotions) and one unruly and appetitive (base desires). Balance, truth and enlightenment come only when reason controls both.

True self = the rational soul: the part that seeks knowledge, contemplates eternal truths and aspires to the realm of the Forms.

St. Augustine of Hippo

  • Influential Christian philosopher and theologian of Late Antiquity; bridged Greek philosophy (especially Plato) with early Christian doctrine.
  • Unity of body and soul. Body = mortal, tied to earthly desires. Soul = immortal, created by God to know and love Him. The soul is superior and its well-being comes first; the body is not evil but must be guided by the soul.
  • The self is the soul in relationship with God. Identity is rooted not in reason alone but in our capacity to love, choose and believe. Fulfillment comes from a personal relationship with God, not from earthly knowledge or pleasure.
“You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our hearts are restless until they rest in you.”
  • Divided will: a result of the Fall of Man. We often know the good but fail to do it because of disordered desires. This inner conflict is concupiscence, the inclination to sin.
  • Echoes Plato's tripartite soul, but grounded in theology: the self is torn between the love of self (cupiditas) and the love of God (caritas).
  • Self-knowledge and divine illumination: agrees with "know thyself" but adds that self-knowledge must lead to God-knowledge, because the soul reflects the image of its Creator. True knowledge is possible only through divine illumination: God enlightens the mind to grasp eternal truths.

True self = the soul oriented toward God; whole through faith, grace and the pursuit of divine truth, not earthly pleasure, reason alone or appearance.

René Descartes

  • French philosopher, mathematician and scientist; the Father of Modern Philosophy. Unified algebra and geometry into analytic geometry.
  • Mind–body dualism: two distinct substances. Body = physical, material, extended in space. Mind = non-physical, immaterial, seat of consciousness and thought. The body can be doubted; the mind is certain because thinking occurs there.
  • The thinking self: the essence of the self is thought. Doubting, understanding, affirming, denying, imagining and willing prove the self exists even if everything else is uncertain.
“I think, therefore I am.” (Cogito, ergo sum)
  • Rationalism: reason, not sensory experience, is the primary source of knowledge. He distrusted the senses (they deceive); clear and distinct ideas reached by reason are the basis of certainty.

True self = the mind, a rational thinking entity; to be human is to think.

John Locke

  • English philosopher and physician; leading thinker of the Enlightenment; Father of Liberalism (individual rights, government by consent).
  • Tabula rasa (blank slate): rejected innate ideas. The mind at birth is blank; all knowledge comes from experience.
  • Empiricism: knowledge comes only from sense experience (see, hear, touch, taste, smell). The mind receives data, forms simple ideas, then combines them into complex ones. Sense experience is the ultimate judge of ideas, directly opposing Rationalism.
  • The self: not an immaterial thinking substance (Descartes) but a continuously developing consciousness shaped by experience, formed by memories of sensory experiences and our reflection on them.

Personal identity = continuity of consciousness, especially through memory.

David Hume

  • Scottish philosopher, historian and economist; Scottish Enlightenment. Pushed empiricism to its logical extremes; skeptical about reason, personal identity and causation.
  • Radical empiricism: the mind begins as a blank slate and receives impressions (vivid sensory experiences), which give rise to ideas (faint copies of impressions). All thoughts and concepts derive from experience.
  • Skepticism about the self: there is no permanent self beyond experiences. The "self" is a bundle of perceptions, a shifting stream of sensations, thoughts, emotions and memories.
“I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe anything but the perception.”
  • Bundle Theory: the self is not a single unified substance but a collection of perceptions connected by habits of thought and memory. We wrongly assume they belong to one unchanging self; it is a mental construct.
  • Causation: we never perceive cause and effect, only one event following another. Belief in causation is habit, not logical certainty.

True self: a fluid, ever-changing collection of experiences with no fixed "I". Personal identity is more a psychological illusion than a metaphysical reality.

Immanuel Kant

  • Central figure of modern philosophy (metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, aesthetics).
  • Reason and morality: morality must be grounded in reason, not religion, emotion or consequences. Moral principles must be discoverable by rational thought, independent of doctrine. To judge what is right, ask: What would reason require of all rational beings in this situation?
ImperativeMeaning
HypotheticalConditional commands based on personal desires or goals
CategoricalUnconditional moral obligations that apply to everyone regardless of desires or consequences

Formulations of the Categorical Imperative

#1 Universality: “Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law.”
#2 Humanity: “Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of another, always at the same time as an end and never merely as a means.”
  • Good Will and moral duty: an action is moral because of its motive, not its result. The only thing good without qualification is the Good Will, the will to do one's duty simply because it is right, out of respect for the moral law, not for benefit or emotional reward.
  • True self: the moral self shows when we act from rational commitment to duty, not instinct, emotion or social pressure. The true self is autonomous (self-governing through reason). To be truly human is to freely choose what is right, guided by principles that apply universally to all rational beings.

Dilemmas used in class

  • Murderer at the door: a murderer asks if your hiding friend is inside. Lie or tell the truth?
  • Trolley problem: pull the lever to divert a trolley and kill one person to save five, or do nothing and let it kill five?

Sigmund Freud

  • Austrian neurologist, philosopher and psychologist; Father of Psychoanalysis (unconscious mind, childhood experiences).
  • Psychoanalysis = a theory of personality and a therapeutic method. People are strongly influenced by unconscious thoughts, feelings, desires and memories, many rooted in early childhood. Therapy aims to bring unconscious material into conscious awareness to resolve deep-seated conflicts and anxieties.

Gilbert Ryle

  • 20th-century British philosopher (Oxford); philosophy of mind; key figure in ordinary language philosophy; known for his critique of Cartesian dualism.
  • Rejected Descartes's view of a material body plus an immaterial mind, calling it the “ghost in the machine”, a misleading metaphor.
  • Category mistake: describing something as if it belongs to a category it does not. Descartes made one by treating the mind as a separate thing parallel to the body instead of seeing mental life as behaviors and dispositions rooted in physical actions.

The self as behavior: not a hidden non-material substance but defined by observable behavior, actions and dispositions. To talk about someone's mind is to talk about how they act, respond and engage with the world.

Paul Churchland

  • Contemporary American philosopher (philosophy of mind, neuroscience, AI); leading advocate of eliminative materialism.
  • Materialism: everything about consciousness (thoughts, emotions, identity) is explained by brain activity. The self is not a non-physical soul; the self is the brain.
  • Eliminative materialism: concepts like beliefs, desires and intentions belong to folk psychology, an outdated, pre-scientific framework.
  • Role of neuroscience: brain scans and neural data give more accurate insight; understanding of consciousness will shift from subjective concepts to scientific models rooted in biology and physics.
  • Self as a neurobiological system: not fixed or permanent but a dynamic, evolving system of neurons shaped by genetics, environment and experience. Personality, memory, identity and consciousness are emergent properties of brain processes.
  • Changes in brain chemistry or structure (injury, drugs, disease) alter the self, so the self is inseparable from the body.

Maurice Merleau-Ponty

  • French phenomenological philosopher (consciousness, perception, embodiment, lived experience); contemporary of Sartre and de Beauvoir; key figure in existential and phenomenological thought.
  • Embodied subjectivity: rejected mind-body dualism. The body is not just a physical object but essential to how we experience the world. The body is the subject: we don't merely have a body, we are our body.
  • The lived body (corps vécu): not something observed from outside but the medium through which we experience the world; the self-in-action. Through it we see, touch, speak, feel joy or pain and make meaning.
  • Perception is the foundation of knowledge: before we think or theorize we experience through the senses. It is active and interpretative, and meaning arises from bodily interaction with the world.

The self = embodied consciousness: a living, sensing, moving body that experiences and interprets the world; neither a disembodied mind nor a mechanical brain. You can't separate thought from movement, emotion from gesture, or identity from physical presence.

Part 2 · Sociological & Anthropological Perspectives on the Self

What Is Sociology?

You should be able to: define sociology; explain the "I" and "Me"; explain the looking-glass self and give real examples; describe the dramaturgical approach; define anthropology and its four branches; define culture; compare self in individualistic vs. collectivistic cultures; describe kapwa, hiya, pakikisama, utang na loob.
  • Sociology: the systematic study of human society and social behavior; how people interact in groups, institutions and cultures; focuses on patterns, relationships and social influences.
  • Society: a group of individuals who share a common culture, live within a defined territory, and interact through shared norms and institutions. Society shapes individual behavior and beliefs.
  • Analyzed through categories: ethnicity, religion, socioeconomic status, gender identity, sexual orientation, age.
  • The sociological perspective: (1) Seeing the general in the particular; (2) Seeing the strange in the familiar.

George Herbert Mead

  • American philosopher, sociologist and psychologist; key figure in symbolic interactionism.
  • The self is not present at birth; it emerges through social interaction, especially communication with symbols (language, gestures).
  • Role-taking: we learn to see ourselves from others' perspectives; central to self-awareness.
  • “I” = spontaneous, impulsive, creative aspect. “Me” = socialized, reflective, rule-following aspect. The self is a dynamic interplay between the two.

Stages of self-development

Generalized other: the internalized sense of society's attitudes, expectations and norms. We use it to evaluate our behavior and align with social norms; crucial for functioning as members of a larger society.

Charles Cooley

  • American sociologist, best known for the Looking-Glass Self: our self-concept is shaped by our perceptions of how others see us.
StepProcess
1We imagine how we appear to others

Erving Goffman

  • Canadian-American sociologist; symbolic interactionism and the presentation of the self in everyday life.
  • Dramaturgical approach: social life is a theatrical performance; people are actors on a stage.
  • The self as performance: not a fixed identity but constructed through social interaction; roles change with context and audience.
  • Front stage: where people perform roles in formal or public settings. Back stage: where they drop public roles and are more authentic or relaxed.
  • Impression management: actively controlling the image we project through appearance, language, gestures and setting, to influence others' perceptions and maintain a desired self-image.
  • Face: the positive social value a person claims for themselves in interactions. Face-work: strategies to maintain dignity and avoid embarrassment.

What Is Anthropology?

  • The scientific study of humanity across time and space: human behavior, biological and cultural evolution, social and belief systems, language and communication. It explores present and past, including fossil records and extinct human species.

Part 3 · Psychological Perspective on the Self

What Is Psychology?

You should be able to: define psychology and its goals; identify major perspectives; explain James's "I" and "Me"; describe Lester's unified vs. multiple self; explain Winnicott's true/false self; describe Rogers's real self, ideal self and congruence; explain Bandura's self-efficacy and the four stages of observational learning.

  • Investigates: motivation, perception, personality, cognition, intelligence, emotion, mental health.

David Lester

  • Existential psychologist and suicidologist; explored the inner nature of the self amid distress, isolation and the search for meaning; focus on the tension between a unified and a fragmented self.
  • Unified self: coherent, stable, consistent; provides grounding, identity and direction; linked to well-being and resilience; self-experience stays steady across roles and situations.
  • Multiple/fragmented self: made of different roles, identities or internal voices that may clash. Not always unhealthy, but can cause distress when unintegrated.
  • Risks of fragmentation: inner conflict and identity confusion; emptiness or isolation; trouble making decisions or keeping stable relationships; may contribute to existential distress or suicidal ideation.
  • Integration and meaning: integrating the multiple selves into a coherent narrative helps people cope with challenges, feel purpose and continuity, and avoid psychological disintegration.

Donald Winnicott

  • English pediatrician and psychoanalyst (object relations theory, developmental psychology). The self develops through early interactions with caregivers, especially the mother; a healthy self feels real, spontaneous and connected, emerging from emotional attunement and care.
  • True self: authentic, spontaneous self that arises when the infant feels safe and accepted; rooted in genuine feelings, creativity and personal experience; develops when the caregiver is “good enough”; allows free expression of needs and emotions.
  • False self: a protective facade developed when the environment doesn't allow authentic expression; the child over-complies to gain approval or avoid rejection; may seem polite or "well-behaved" but feels empty or disconnected.
  • Good-enough parenting: responsive and attuned but allows gradual frustration; helps the child move from total dependence to independence; creates safe space for self-expression.
  • Holding environment: the emotional and physical environment that “holds” the child psychologically; gives security, continuity and containment so the child can explore, take emotional risks and form identity.

Carl Rogers

  • American psychologist; a founder of humanistic psychology. Each person has a self-concept shaped by life experiences, especially early relationships. Stressed self-acceptance, personal growth and self-actualization.
  • Self-concept: the organized, consistent set of beliefs and perceptions about oneself: self-image (how you see yourself), ideal self (how you wish to be), self-worth (how much you value yourself).
  • Congruence: self-image aligns with experiences and ideal self → psychological health. Incongruence: mismatch between real experiences and self-concept → anxiety, confusion, low self-esteem.
  • Self-actualization: the innate, lifelong drive to grow, fulfill potential and become one's true self; possible only in an environment of psychological safety and acceptance.
  • Conditions of worth: feeling valued only when meeting expectations (smart, obedient) → incongruence and a false self. Unconditional positive regard: being accepted and loved without conditions → the true self can emerge and grow.

Albert Bandura

  • Canadian-American psychologist (Stanford); social cognitive theory, therapy, personality; helped bridge behaviorism and cognitive psychology.
  • The self is a socially constructed, self-reflective, self-regulating system. Social Learning Theory (later Social Cognitive Theory): we form the self by observing others, reflecting on experiences and evaluating our own capabilities.
  • Reciprocal determinism: personal factors (thoughts, self-perceptions), behavior and environment all influence one another; the self is shaped through constant back-and-forth with surroundings.

Self-efficacy

Our belief in our ability to succeed in specific situations. Shaped by:

  • Mastery experiences: doing something successfully
  • Vicarious experiences: seeing others succeed
  • Social persuasion: encouragement or discouragement
  • Emotional and physiological states: how we feel about a task
